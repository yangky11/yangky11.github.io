What Happens When Formalization Becomes Cheap?

A personal reflection on eight years of AI and formalization

Kaiyu Yang September 22, 2026 20 min read

I started working on machine learning for formal theorem proving in 2018. When people ask how I got into the field so early, I have sometimes given an answer that makes me sound quite visionary.

The actual story is that my advisor had a student leaving, and he assigned the project to me. My apologies to everyone who got the visionary version.

It was a fortunate assignment. Over the following years, I developed CoqGym and LeanDojo and contributed to Goedel-Prover. I was lucky to join a small research community and watch its ideas become widely recognized. For much of that time, I was an enthusiastic proponent of formalization as a way to make AI’s outputs verifiable and trustworthy.

The hope was appealing: AI could do the creative work, while a rigorous formal system, such as Lean, checked it. A major obstacle was the labor of expressing everything precisely enough in Lean. Now coding agents are taking over more of that labor. Codex and Claude Code are now the strongest systems for working in Lean—a striking change from a year ago. Using Lean’s feedback, they can formalize major results like Fermat’s Last Theorem and build large software projects with specifications and correctness proofs.1

That progress has made another question harder to ignore: once we have formalized a claim and proved it in Lean, how much of the original problem have we actually solved? Sometimes the answer is a great deal. Sometimes successful formalization leaves the important problem largely unresolved.

1 Coding agents and Fermat

In Anthropic’s September 2026 formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem, Claude Code agents worked largely autonomously for 11 days to produce a complete Lean proof—a formalization project previously expected to take years.

This distinction matters especially now. Recent security incidents involving AI agents, and the debate about pacing frontier AI development, make formal verification look like an attractive part of the solution.2 I agree that it could be. But my experience has made me more cautious about how much assurance we should expect from it.

I keep having versions of this conversation with friends, collaborators, and students thinking about research topics to work on. This blog is an attempt to explain how my beliefs changed over eight years, and why formalization is no longer my sole research focus. Some of these views are speculative. I have been wrong before and may be wrong again. I’d be happy to compare notes, even if we end up disagreeing.

2 Security and pacing the frontier

OpenAI and Anthropic have reported AI agents gaining unauthorized access to real systems during research evaluations.

Both OpenAI and Anthropic have committed to embedded independent evaluators as part of their pacing efforts. See also the employee-led Pacing the Frontier statement.

What a formal guarantee tells us

The appeal of formalization is that we can check an AI’s work without having to trust the AI. Whether it gives us a mathematical proof or a piece of software, we can ask it to spell out its claims, assumptions, and reasoning in a form a computer can check.

Suppose we want a program that sorts a list of numbers. Its output should be in ascending order and contain exactly the same numbers, including repetitions. Those two requirements are its specification.

For years, the labor of writing all this down and getting the proofs checked was the obstacle that dominated my thinking.

How AI changed the cost

Early on, even getting neural models to produce useful formal proofs reliably was a research problem. Open tools and datasets, including our CoqGym and LeanDojo projects, helped more researchers work on it.

One successful approach was post-training: take an existing language model and give it more training on Lean statements and proofs. Goedel-Prover (2025) followed this approach, building a large collection of generated and checked Lean proofs and using them to train increasingly capable specialized provers.

The results across the field were impressive. In 2024, Google DeepMind’s AlphaProof used Lean to solve problems from the International Mathematical Olympiad, a competition for some of the world’s strongest young mathematicians. Together with AlphaGeometry 2, it reached silver-medal-level performance. The following year, ByteDance reported silver-medal-level performance with its Lean-based Seed-Prover system. AI could find arguments requiring substantial mathematical insight and produce proofs that a computer could check.

The bigger shift is that the strongest systems for working in Lean are now general coding agents such as Codex and Claude Code.

They often substantially outperform models built specifically for theorem proving. The tools people use to build ordinary software have also become exceptionally good at formalization.

In retrospect, I think this was to be expected. Frontier models have a higher ceiling for the reasoning the task requires. Being fluent in Lean helps, but the model still needs to find a mathematical argument or understand why a program should work. Lean’s feedback can guide that reasoning; it cannot supply all the missing insight.3

General coding agents also bring a broader set of skills and tools. They can navigate a codebase, search documentation, write small programs to test an idea, and coordinate changes across many files. All of this matters when the task grows from proving an isolated theorem to formalizing a large mathematical development or verifying a software project.

3 Reasoning and formal feedback

OpenAI reports that its agents reached a solution to the Navier–Stokes problem, followed by a separate stage of Lean formalization and verification.

More importantly, general models can acquire the same specialized skills. If we can construct useful training data for a domain, frontier labs can use it to teach that domain to their strongest models. They can also train specialist models and transfer those skills into a general model.4 Lean expertise can become one capability among many.5

For users of formalization, the result is that much more of a project can be delegated. In Anthropic’s Fermat’s Last Theorem project, Claude Code agents formalized an existing human proof in 11 days, working largely autonomously on a project previously expected to take years. In software, agents can develop implementations, specifications, and correctness proofs together across large codebases.

That is the sense in which I mean formalization is becoming cheap: far less specialist human labor is needed to get a mathematical argument or software property into a checked form. Compute and libraries still matter. But the automation now covers much more of the work that used to require an expert at the keyboard.

In that practical sense, I sometimes call proof search and parts of autoformalization effectively solved. For many well-specified problems within the reasoning ability of a frontier model, the labor of formalization is no longer the main obstacle.

This is much of what I hoped for when I entered the field. If the labor of formalization were the main obstacle to trustworthy software, this progress should bring us much closer to that goal. A recent project made me look more carefully at that “if.”

4 Learning from specialists

One mechanism is on-policy distillation: a model generates its own attempts and learns from an expert model’s predictions along the way. Specialized training can therefore contribute to a general model’s capabilities.

5 Can a specialist keep its advantage?

The source of a specialist’s advantage matters. If your Lean data comes from public resources such as mathlib or from a readily reproducible pipeline for generating problems and proofs, OpenAI or Anthropic can build similar data internally. Having that data first can be valuable, but it does not by itself give a separate Lean model a lasting advantage.

My guess is that much of the earlier advantage of models such as Goedel-Prover came from giving Lean the attention it had not yet received in OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s main training runs. If that was the gap, it was unlikely to last once the labs became interested.

What VeriTile changed for me

VeriTile, our project led by Zenan Li, uses Lean to verify GPU kernels written in Triton: the small, performance-critical programs that do much of the numerical work in AI training.

We thought kernels were an unusually promising application of formal verification. We also had a larger possibility in mind: they could be an important part of recursive self-improvement, with AI improving the infrastructure used to train future AI.

Imagine an AI optimizing its own GPU kernels, proving that its changes are correct, and using the faster kernels to train the next model. Formal verification could make that loop much easier to trust.

Why kernels looked so promising

Training a large model is expensive and difficult to debug. A program can keep running while quietly doing the wrong thing, with an error only becoming visible much later as a loss spike or poorer training results. By then, finding the cause can be harder than fixing it.

Performance also matters enormously. Engineers reorganize computations and combine operations to make better use of the GPU. A faster kernel can save time throughout a training run. But every optimization raises the same question: did we preserve the behavior we care about?

Kernels seemed to offer a good way to answer that question. They are relatively compact, and their intended computations appear mathematical. We can often write a slow, straightforward reference implementation, then ask whether a faster one does the same thing. Here it seemed we could specify exactly what “correct” meant.

The hope was that AI could optimize these kernels and prove the replacements correct, giving us speed and confidence without requiring a formal-methods expert to scrutinize every change.

What we could prove

VeriTile gives this idea a concrete form. It represents Triton kernels inside Lean, with a precise meaning for each operation. A kernel can then be checked against a mathematical specification or compared with a reference kernel. Proof agents use this framework to construct proofs, with Lean checking their work.

A more substantial example is FlashAttention, which makes the attention computation in language models more efficient. A straightforward implementation of attention creates a large array of attention scores. FlashAttention works through smaller blocks and updates intermediate results as it goes, avoiding that large array and reducing how much data moves through GPU memory. The code looks quite different from the formula it implements.

VeriTile proves that its FlashAttention kernel computes the same mathematical function as a straightforward reference implementation, using a model of arithmetic over the real numbers.6 It covers every valid input under the theorem’s assumptions, including inputs nobody thought to test.

6 The FlashAttention guarantee

The project documentation and proof source describe equivalence between VeriTile’s FlashAttention-1 forward kernel and a separately verified naive kernel, including causal and noncausal variants. This result uses real-number arithmetic; it does not establish identical floating-point outputs on a GPU.

These proofs can rule out whole classes of implementation mistakes that testing might miss. But my motivating question was more practical: can we use the faster kernel in a long training run without harming the outcome?

When exact equivalence asks for too much

If the faster kernel produces the same output for every input, down to the last bit, and otherwise behaves the same way, the rest of training cannot tell the difference. A proof of this bitwise equivalence, using an accurate model of execution, would justify replacing the reference kernel.

Many useful optimizations, however, change how arithmetic is grouped. Floating-point calculations round intermediate results, so addition is not associative: changing the grouping can change the result.

This prints 1.0 0.0, although both expressions equal 2 in real arithmetic. Grouping the additions into independent pairs creates an opportunity for parallel execution, but also changes the result.

Demanding bitwise equivalence would rule out some useful optimizations. A difference in output does not, by itself, tell us that an optimization is bad for training. But neither can we declare every small difference harmless.

A more faithful floating-point model could describe those differences, but it would not make the two computations equal. A better prover cannot prove a false statement. We need a different claim, perhaps a bound on the numerical difference.

How small is small enough for training?

Even if we could prove an error bound for every relevant input, we would still need to know which bound is sufficient for training. Asking an AI to choose one does not remove the need to justify it.

A local difference can interact with optimizer state and many subsequent computations over a long training run. To turn a bound on that difference into useful assurance, we need a connection between the kernel’s numerical behavior and the training outcome we want to protect.

That connection may also depend on the training data. There may be no useful worst-case guarantee across all datasets; the practical question may be whether an optimization works for the particular dataset we have. For the systems and optimizations we cared about, machine learning theory did not give us a practically useful answer.

In practice, engineers compare training trajectories on relevant data, using both optimized kernels and a slower trusted reference. These experiments are expensive and cannot guarantee what will happen in the eventual full-scale run, but they test the behavior we care about more directly. Sometimes engineers disable an optimization and accept slower training when they cannot get enough confidence in its consequences.

For the self-improvement loop we imagined, proofs still remove real sources of uncertainty. But we may need expensive training experiments before adopting an AI’s faster kernel, even after Lean has checked a proof of mathematical equivalence.

This was the important update for me. We knew what we wanted: faster training without harming the result. VeriTile proved useful properties, but for optimizations that change numerical behavior, we did not know how to turn those properties into the assurance we wanted.

A missing specification is sometimes an unfinished document. In this case, it can be a missing piece of science.

Better AI may help us develop the missing theory too. But that scientific progress is a different achievement from automating formalization; it does not follow simply from having a model that is very good at Lean.

The work before the proof

With VeriTile, we knew what we wanted but lacked a useful mathematical connection to it. Sometimes the work starts even earlier: we have not settled what we want.

Consider smart contracts: programs on a blockchain that can control real money. Bugs can be expensive and difficult to fix after deployment. This looks like an excellent application for formal verification, and tools such as the Certora Prover already let developers check properties of their contracts.

My conversations with smart-contract auditing companies have given me a more nuanced picture. Their work draws on human review, static analysis, testing, fuzzing, and formal verification. In most of the companies I have spoken with, formal verification is only a small part of that work.

A lot of the work is figuring out what the client actually wants the system to do. The clients themselves often do not have a clear answer at the outset. Knowing that you want to protect users’ money does not tell you exactly what to prove. Arriving at a useful specification is part of the audit, and making proofs cheaper does not automatically resolve that work.

For a long time, the last two steps consumed so much effort that they dominated my thinking. AI is rapidly reducing that effort. The first two are becoming harder to ignore.

This is not a new observation. What changed for me is how much weight I put on it. I used to expect that making formalization sufficiently easy would make these remaining problems manageable across a broad range of applications. I now expect them to determine where formalization delivers the most value.

AI can help with the first two steps too: clarify a request, propose a model, or spot an omission. But we still need evidence that its choices are good. A proof can establish that code meets an AI-generated specification without establishing that the specification captures what we want. That distinction matters especially when agents act on our behalf.

When is a proof worth it?

Part of the appeal of formal verification is the promise of closure: we have checked this, and we can move on. That is a different proposition from “we ran more tests and found fewer bugs.” But in many applications, the proof closes a narrower question than the one we actually care about.

A proof covers every case under its stated assumptions; passing a collection of tests does not. That distinction matters. But the practical benefit depends on whether ruling out the failures covered by the proof changes what we can confidently build or deploy.

Even a complete guarantee would have to justify its cost. When the guarantee is partial, we need to be clear about which benefit we are paying for.7 Formal verification belongs in the same practical comparison as testing, fuzzing, code review, and experiments: which uncertainties matter, and what is the best way to address them? For VeriTile, a proof of mathematical equivalence removed one concern, but left us needing expensive training experiments before adopting a numerically different kernel. The theorem was useful without settling the decision.

7 Selling formal methods

Mike Dodds’s account of selling formal methods at Galois describes the same practical pressure: verification projects compete with other uses of a client’s resources. He also describes how technical caveats get lost as results travel from engineers to management and marketing. A precise guarantee can become a much broader promise in the telling.

The strongest applications combine high stakes with a close connection between mathematical properties and practical requirements. Hardware is a natural example: we can ask whether a circuit implements the intended arithmetic. Low-level systems software offers others: preserving program behavior through a compiler optimization, enforcing isolation in an OS kernel, or ensuring that a database transaction happens completely or not at all. Regulated industries may offer further opportunities where precise requirements and evidence are part of getting a product into use.

This leaves me more optimistic about formal verification as an everyday tool than as a universal guarantee. High stakes help justify expensive verification. As formalization gets cheaper, finding a few important bugs or proving one useful property can be enough to justify it.

Jane Street’s recent change of view illustrates how cheaper proofs and more AI-generated code can change the economics. I expect formalization to become a routine part of coding-agent workflows, alongside testing. Boris Cherny, the creator of Claude Code, recently reported that a few short prompts to Opus 5.5, using Lean, produced 16 pull requests fixing bugs and race conditions in the Claude Agent SDK. The concrete payoff he describes is finding and fixing bugs. That is valuable even without a claim that the entire SDK is now correct.

An agent might prove a property of one component and use property-based tests—checking a general property on many generated inputs—elsewhere. Lower costs make it easier to use formal methods wherever they help, without requiring complete verification to be the destination of every project.

What about AI for mathematics?

Mathematics has an unusually direct connection to formal statements: the theorem itself is often what we care about. If a mathematical result matters to you, establishing it rigorously has value in its own right.

For AI research, mathematics has also been a way to demonstrate what models can do. If the whole pitch is “look, AI can do math,” I think that particular party is largely over.

Can formal verification make AI safer?

Recent security incidents make verified sandboxes an appealing idea. If an agent can find vulnerabilities that humans miss, perhaps we should put it behind boundaries that we can prove it cannot cross. This is one application of formal verification that I find compelling.

A sound proof can rule out an entire class of attacks, under its assumptions. The attacker becoming smarter does not invalidate the theorem. We do not have to anticipate every attack to benefit from a guarantee that untrusted code cannot access protected memory.

For a particular deployment, two questions remain. Does the running system enforce the boundary? A proof about sandboxed code may rely on assumptions about the runtime and the underlying machine. Those assumptions need to hold in the deployed system. We also need to account for the agent’s other tools. If another tool can retrieve the same data for the agent, the memory guarantee does not close that route.8

8 Checking agent permissions

NVIDIA’s OpenShell research checks proposed agent permissions against an expert-approved policy. The guarantee covers the policy model, not the entire sandbox.

Are the restrictions sufficient for the task? Even a perfectly enforced boundary leaves the agent able to act within it. An agent that cannot read protected memory may still produce a harmful code change that a user then applies. The memory guarantee remains useful, but we need other safeguards for that permitted channel. Deciding which actions to allow, and what checks they require, brings us back to intent and the consequences of getting it wrong.

The debate about pacing frontier AI development makes these distinctions consequential. Verified safeguards could provide important evidence for deploying a more capable system. To judge whether that evidence is sufficient, we need to know which failures the proofs rule out, whether their assumptions hold, and what remains to be addressed through testing, monitoring, or other safeguards. “Formally verified” alone does not tell us whether it is safe to proceed.

What this changes for my research

I expect formalization to become more widely used as it gets cheaper. I also expect it to settle a smaller fraction of the questions I most want to study. After eight years working on this topic, I find those expectations surprisingly compatible.

I am increasingly interested in how humans can justifiably trust AI’s outputs when a complete formal guarantee is out of reach. Humans are weak, compute-limited verifiers: we may be unable to solve the same problems ourselves, and we cannot check everything an AI produces. How can stronger systems earn our trust despite that gap? One approach is to train them to make their work easier for weaker verifiers to check, as explored in prover–verifier games. I am also interested in the institutions—rules, incentives, and accountability—that allow humans and AI agents to trust one another and cooperate while preserving meaningful human control.

I do not have a settled research agenda for all of this. Formalization will remain part of my work, and so will the habits it taught me: asking exactly what a claim says, what it assumes, and what would count as evidence for it. What has changed is how much I expect a formal guarantee to settle. I still want AI’s work to be trustworthy. I am less convinced that formalization alone will get us there.
